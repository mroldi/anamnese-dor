1. Background

Myofascial pain syndrome (MPS), characterised by the presence of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs), is recognised as one of the most prevalent causes of musculoskeletal pain in clinical practice.1,5 Clinical diagnosis is based on standardised palpation to identify taut muscle bands and referred pain patterns.1

According to the IASP taxonomy, chronic pain is classified as nociceptive, neuropathic, or nociplastic. MPS has traditionally been categorised within the nociceptive phenotype; however, accumulating evidence indicates frequent co-occurrence with neuropathic or nociplastic mechanisms — constituting the mixed-pain phenotype.2,3,6

In orthopedic settings, structural diagnoses may overshadow the MPS component, rendering it a clinically hidden yet functionally significant contributor to pain burden and treatment failure.4 Recognition of MPS is therapeutically critical, as its management diverges substantially from structural or neuropathically-directed approaches.2,3

2. Objectives

To determine the prevalence and anatomical distribution of MPS among patients with chronic pain referred to an interdisciplinary pain clinic within a quaternary orthopedic teaching hospital; and to characterise the co-occurrence of MPS and neuropathic pain (mixed-pain phenotype) in this population.

3. Methods
Design Retrospective, single-centre observational study
Setting IPC, IOT, HCFMUSP — quaternary-level public teaching hospital, São Paulo, Brazil
Population 91 consecutive chronic pain patients referred from multiple orthopedic subspecialties
Variables Demographics; pain location; pain type (nociceptive / neuropathic / nociplastic / mixed); MPS diagnosis; prior treatments
Assessment Standardised comprehensive musculoskeletal and neurological examination, including systematic MTrP assessment by palpation, in all patients
MPS Criteria MPS diagnosed by systematic palpation per Simons, Travell & Simons (1999): (i) palpable taut band; (ii) hypersensitive trigger point (MTrP) within the band; (iii) reproduction of the patient's familiar pain on compression; (iv) characteristic referred pain pattern. Active and latent MTrPs distinguished. Consistent with IASP nociceptive pain taxonomy (2021).
Statistics Descriptive statistics only (frequencies, proportions). No inferential analysis — exploratory cross-sectional single-centre design.

★ Principal Finding MPS was identified in 76.9% of chronic orthopedic pain patients and constituted the predominant musculoskeletal co-diagnosis across all anatomical regions evaluated. The highest prevalence was observed in patients with spinal pain (95.2%), in whom MPS was nearly universal. This pattern confirms MPS as a clinically significant yet systematically under-recognised condition in orthopedic referral populations.

5. Conclusions
  • 1 MPS was identified in 76.9% of chronic pain patients in a quaternary orthopedic setting (70/91), confirming it as a high-prevalence, yet frequently overlooked, clinical entity.
  • 2 MPS prevalence was high across anatomical locations: spinal (95.2%), shoulder (72.7%), and lower limb (72.7%), underscoring its transregional nature as a co-diagnosis.
  • 3 Among 27 patients (29.7%) with neuropathic pain, 70% also presented MPS, establishing the mixed-pain phenotype as a common clinical reality.
  • 4 The bidirectional overlap (~27% of MPS patients with neuropathic component) is consistent with the hypothesis that peripheral MTrP input may sustain or co-occur with central sensitisation mechanisms.6
  • 5 Systematic MTrP assessment by palpation should be incorporated into routine evaluation of all chronic pain patients referred to orthopedic subspecialties, to prevent under-diagnosis and treatment failure.2,3,4
Limitations

Sample size: n = 91 is appropriate for a single-centre prevalence study, but limits subgroup analysis — e.g., per-subspecialty MPS rates should be interpreted with caution.

Design: Retrospective, cross-sectional, single-centre; findings reflect a quaternary orthopedic referral population and may not generalise to primary care settings.

Overlap: Pain-location subgroups are not mutually exclusive (patients may present with pain in multiple regions), limiting direct prevalence comparisons.

Diagnosis: MPS diagnosis relied on clinical palpation criteria without instrumental confirmation (e.g., ultrasound or EMG), consistent with current standard practice.
