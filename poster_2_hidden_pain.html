◉ Background

Myofascial pain syndrome (MPS), characterized by myofascial trigger points (TrPs), is among the most common causes of pain in musculoskeletal practice. Diagnosis is clinical, based on muscle palpation and referred pain patterns.1,5

Under the current IASP pain classification, pain is categorized as nociceptive, neuropathic, or nociplastic. MPS has traditionally been a nociceptive condition; however, growing evidence supports that TrP pain can coexist with neuropathic or nociplastic mechanisms — constituting a mixed-pain phenotype.2,3,6

In orthopedic settings, musculoskeletal pain often carries a dominant nociceptive or structural label, potentially masking the MPS component. Recognition of MPS is critical because its treatment diverges from purely structural interventions.4

⚙ Objectives

To investigate the prevalence and distribution of myofascial pain syndrome (MPS) and mixed pain in a cohort of chronic pain patients referred to an Interdisciplinary Pain Clinic within a quaternary orthopedic teaching hospital, and to describe the association of MPS with neuropathic pain.

🔬 Methods

Design: Retrospective observational study. Review of medical records from an Interdisciplinary Pain Clinic, Institute of Orthopedics, quaternary teaching hospital, São Paulo, Brazil.

Population: 91 consecutive chronic pain patients referred from multiple orthopedic subspecialties (spinal unit, shoulder, knee, hand, lower limb).

Variables: Demographics, clinical diagnoses, pain type (nociceptive / neuropathic / nociplastic / mixed), pain location, MPS diagnosis, prior treatments and clinical outcomes.

Diagnostic approach: Detailed history plus comprehensive musculoskeletal and neuropathic examination in all patients. MPS diagnosis based on clinical criteria including TrP identification by palpation.1

✔ Conclusions
  • MPS was identified in 76.9% of chronic orthopedic pain patients, confirming it as a pervasive but frequently overlooked condition in this setting.
  • MPS prevalence was notably high across pain locations: spinal (95.2%), shoulder (72.7%), and lower limb (72.7%).
  • In patients with neuropathic pain, 70% also had MPS — highlighting the mixed-pain phenotype as a common clinical reality requiring simultaneous management of both components.
  • Conversely, 27% of MPS patients also presented a neuropathic component, consistent with the concept that peripheral TrP input can drive or coexist with central sensitization.6
  • Systematic musculoskeletal examination is essential in all orthopedic chronic pain patients to avoid under-diagnosing MPS and missing mixed pain phenotypes that require a multimodal, mechanism-targeted approach.2,3,4
