1. Background

Myofascial pain syndrome (MPS), characterised by the presence of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs), is recognised as one of the most prevalent causes of musculoskeletal pain in clinical practice.1,5 Clinical diagnosis is based on standardised palpation techniques to identify taut muscle bands and associated referred pain patterns.1

According to the IASP taxonomy, chronic pain is classified as nociceptive, neuropathic, or nociplastic. MPS has traditionally been categorised within the nociceptive phenotype; however, accumulating evidence indicates its frequent co-occurrence with neuropathic or nociplastic mechanisms — constituting what is now termed the mixed-pain phenotype.2,3,6

In orthopedic settings, structural diagnoses (e.g., degenerative joint disease, ligamentous injuries, post-surgical states) may overshadow the MPS component, rendering it a clinically hidden yet functionally significant contributor to pain burden and treatment failure.4 Recognition of MPS is therapeutically critical, as its management — including targeted MTrP interventions — diverges substantially from purely structural or neuropathically-directed approaches.2,3

2. Objectives

To determine the prevalence and anatomical distribution of MPS among patients with chronic pain referred to an interdisciplinary pain clinic within a quaternary orthopedic teaching hospital; and to characterise the co-occurrence of MPS and neuropathic pain (mixed-pain phenotype) in this population.

3. Methods
Design Retrospective, single-centre observational study
Setting Interdisciplinary Pain Clinic (IPC), IOT, HCFMUSP — quaternary-level public teaching hospital, São Paulo, Brazil
Population 91 consecutive chronic pain patients referred from multiple orthopedic subspecialties
Variables Demographics; clinical diagnoses; pain location; pain type (nociceptive / neuropathic / nociplastic / mixed); MPS diagnosis; prior treatments and clinical outcomes
Assessment Standardised comprehensive musculoskeletal and neurological examination, including systematic MTrP assessment by palpation, performed in all patients by the interdisciplinary pain team
Orthopedic Subspecialties Represented

Principal Finding MPS was identified in 76.9% of chronic orthopedic pain patients and constituted the predominant musculoskeletal co-diagnosis across all anatomical regions evaluated. The highest prevalence was observed in patients with spinal pain (95.2%), in whom MPS was nearly universal. This pattern confirms MPS as a clinically significant yet systematically under-recognised condition in orthopedic referral populations.

5. Conclusions
  • 1 MPS was identified in 76.9% of chronic pain patients in a quaternary orthopedic setting (70/91), confirming it as a high-prevalence, yet frequently overlooked, clinical entity across orthopedic subspecialties.
  • 2 MPS prevalence was notably high across anatomical locations: spinal pain (95.2%), shoulder pain (72.7%), and lower limb pain (72.7%), underscoring the transregional nature of MPS as a co-diagnosis in orthopedic populations.
  • 3 Among 27 patients (29.7%) with a neuropathic pain component, 70% also presented MPS, establishing the mixed-pain phenotype as a common clinical reality in this context.
  • 4 The bidirectional overlap — with approximately 27% of MPS patients also exhibiting a neuropathic component — is consistent with the hypothesis that peripheral MTrP input may sustain or co-occur with central sensitisation mechanisms.6
  • 5 Systematic musculoskeletal examination, including MTrP assessment by palpation, should be incorporated into the routine evaluation of all chronic pain patients referred to orthopedic subspecialties, in order to avoid under-diagnosis and treatment failure in the mixed-pain subgroup.2,3,4
